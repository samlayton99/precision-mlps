# Summary: findings orthogonal and relevant to the QI paper

The workshop paper ("Constructing Machine-Precision Neural Networks with Quasi-Interpolants") already does three things: it gives the explicit QI construction (Section 3), it shows that end-to-end training plateaus 6-10 orders above the fp64 floor while a fixed-geometry coefficient fit reaches it (Section 4.1-4.2), and it diagnoses three violations in trained networks -- $\gamma$ stays $O(1)$ so $\lambda=\gamma h\to 0$, outer weights grow rather than staying $O(1)$, and features rank-saturate -- while noting that endpoint loss-Hessian curvature does not explain the gap (Fig. 7), concluding that optimization, not expressivity, is the bottleneck. Everything below is orthogonal to that paper in method and relevant to it in target: rather than studying the trained network as a whole, this project decomposes the MLP into geometry (centers, $\gamma$) $\to$ frozen $\tanh$ features $\Phi$ $\to$ linear readout, and probes each block with exact or least-squares solves on fixed/known geometry rather than with end-to-end gradient training. That decompositional stance is what lets the work convert the paper's three qualitative violations into sharper, mechanism-level statements about which block actually controls precision.

The first orthogonal finding concerns the readout block, which the paper never analyzes on its own. On a fixed correct geometry the readout is a convex least-squares solve that is fast and fp64-accurate, and at equal arithmetic precision it matches or beats the QI convolution formula (lstsq fp64 reaches $\sim 10^{-13}$ versus QI fp64 $\sim 10^{-12}$, both $\sim 10^{-15}$ in mpmath; exp03). More importantly, the readout is underdetermined: the augmented feature matrix $[\Phi,\mathbf{1}]$ carries a roughly 100-dimensional null space at width $N=128$, so the QI and least-squares weight vectors differ by $O(1)$ to $O(10^2)$ per coefficient yet agree as functions to $\sim 10^{-12}$ -- the entire disagreement lives in directions the data cannot see (exp04). This reframes the paper's "weight blowup" violation as benign representational freedom on the correct geometry rather than a fundamental barrier, with min-norm least squares a clean canonical choice. A second, negative result sharpens the paper's Fig. 4: that figure reaches the floor by Adam-then-SSBroyden (a quasi-Newton method) on the well-conditioned cardinal basis, and the project shows that the same readout in raw $\tanh$ coordinates is unreachable by first-order descent (exp12 stalls; only a direct solve or a cardinal/quasi-Newton route works), turning "optimization is the bottleneck" into the more precise "raw coordinates plus first-order optimization is the bottleneck."

The second cluster isolates the geometry block, which the paper only ever fixes wholesale. Varying centers and sampling independently shows that precision is controlled almost entirely by uniform center placement: only uniform centers reach the fp64 floor ($\sim 10^{-13}$ to $10^{-14}$, flat in width), while random, clustered, regular-clustered, and even centers extracted from a trained network all plateau 2-3 orders higher and never descend toward the floor (exp07), whereas the choice of sample points barely matters and $x$-jitter is harmless (exp08). The decisive orthogonal point is that conditioning does not discriminate: uniform and random placements sit at the same $\mathrm{cond}(\Phi)\sim 10^{19}$-$10^{20}$ yet differ in accuracy by orders, which extends the paper's "curvature does not explain the gap" (Fig. 7, a statement about the loss Hessian) to the feature matrix from a second, independent direction. Two further findings here bound and gently challenge the paper: noise sets a hard statistical floor at the noise magnitude with only $1/\sqrt{n}$ recovery (exp08), establishing that the construction's noise-free assumption is load-bearing for any precision claim; and the optimal bandwidth for least squares is adaptive -- pinned near $\lambda\approx 0.30$ for QI but rising with target frequency and falling with width for lstsq, so $\gamma$ tracks the target's bandwidth rather than the grid size (exp06). The last point suggests, though does not yet establish, that the paper's "$\gamma$ must grow as $O(N)$" prediction may be a feature of the construction's fixed-$\lambda$ design rather than a strict precision requirement.

The final orthogonal contribution is dimensional: the paper is entirely 1D, and the project shows the fixed-geometry-plus-least-squares recipe carries to $\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$ via a Radon/tangent-line hexagonal geometry, where each $\tanh$ ridge is pinned tangent to a circle at a hex-packed point, so a scalar center becomes a (direction, offset) pair in the line domain (exp10, exp11). Smooth targets descend with width (e.g. a Gaussian bump to $7.4\times 10^{-11}$ at $N=271$) with the same U-shaped bandwidth tradeoff and the same underdetermination as 1D, though they have not yet reached the fp64 floor at tested widths as $\mathrm{cond}(\Phi)$ grows into the $10^{16}$-$10^{17}$ range, and high-frequency or steep targets remain resolution-limited. Taken together, the body of work is orthogonal to the paper in that it studies the solvable linear structure underneath the trained network rather than the trained network itself, and relevant in that it localizes the paper's open problem with much greater precision: the difficulty is entirely the inner-layer geometry and specifically center placement, the readout is a solved convex (if underdetermined) problem, conditioning is ruled out as the discriminating quantity from two directions, the precision claim is bounded by a quantified noise floor, and the principle generalizes beyond one dimension. The honest boundary -- and the reason the paper's question is localized rather than closed -- is that the pivotal test, whether a standard optimizer can discover the precision-admitting geometry from a random start rather than being handed it, has not yet been run; the one in-data hint (trained-extracted centers behaving like the other non-uniform placements) leans negative.
