# Related work: has this been done before?

Literature check, 2026-09-08, on the question "has anyone solved the bandwidth-versus-precision problem for lattice least-squares approximation, and are there established tools for solving at other precisions?" Short answer: yes on both counts, for the Gaussian kernel. The closest prior work is Adcock, Huybrechs and Piret (2022/2024), which contains the same setup, the same scaling law, an explicit constant, and a rigorous version of the two-wall bound. What is not in the literature, as far as this check found, is the integrated-kernel case (tanh, gelu, swish; kernel order $r\ge1$) and the exact, constant-free floor with the $(\theta/2\pi)^r$ attenuation.

## 1. The same problem, solved for Gaussians: Adcock, Huybrechs, Piret

B. Adcock, D. Huybrechs, C. Piret, "Stable and accurate least squares radial basis function approximations on bounded domains", SIAM J. Numer. Anal. 62(6), 2698–2718 (2024); arXiv:2211.12598 (2022). Read from the arXiv PDF, Sections 1–4.

Their setup, in our vocabulary:

- Gaussian RBF $\phi(r)=e^{-r^2}$ with shape parameter $\varepsilon$, centers on an equispaced lattice $\xi_n=nT/N$ on $[-T,T]$, domain $\Omega\subset(-T,T)$ with radius $B<T$. Centers outside $\Omega$ are our halo; $T-B$ is its width.
- Discrete least squares on $M>N$ samples ("oversampling", they use $M\approx2N$), solved by truncated SVD with an absolute threshold $\tau$: our `gelsd` with `cond`.
- **Linear scaling $\varepsilon=cN$ is optimal in 1-D**, giving "constant overlap between neighbouring RBFs regardless of their number". With grid spacing $h=T/N$ this is $\varepsilon h=cT$ constant, i.e. our $\lambda=\gamma h$ constant. Sublinear scaling $\varepsilon\sim cN^\alpha$ converges to zero but at the suboptimal rate $O(N^{-\alpha k})$ with a growing coefficient norm.
- **An explicit constant.** Their Theorem 8 and eq. (21): $c^\ast=\pi/\big(T\sqrt{2\log(1+\tau^{-2})}\big)$, so $\lambda^\ast=c^\ast T=\pi/\sqrt{2\log(1+\tau^{-2})}$; for $\tau=10^{-16}$ this is $\lambda^\ast=0.259$ for the Gaussian.
- **The saturation error.** Their bound carries the term $1/\sqrt{e^{N^2\pi^2/(2T^2\varepsilon^2)}-1}\approx e^{-\pi^2/(4\lambda^2)}$ (Lemma 5, Theorem 7), obtained by Poisson-type reasoning on the Fourier transform of an extension of $f$ to the whole line (their Lemmas 4–6 use exactly the "extend $f$ to $\mathbb R$, analyze the lattice series in Fourier space" device of expC09).
- **The two-wall structure as a theorem.** Their eq. (7), from Adcock–Huybrechs "Frames and numerical approximation" (SIAM Review 61, 2019, Theorem 1.3): the truncated-SVD least-squares error satisfies $\|f-\mathcal T_N\lambda^{(\tau)}\|\lesssim\inf_z\{\|f-\mathcal T_Nz\|+\tau\|z\|\}$. Approximation error plus threshold times coefficient norm, minimized jointly. This is what our two-wall note tried to state; the difference is that theirs is an infimum over all coefficient vectors (hence a theorem), whereas ours evaluated both terms at the band-edge tone with a declared constant, which the skeptic review showed is violated as a level.

How our constant compares. Our Gaussian rule $A(\lambda)=e^{-\pi^2/\lambda^2}=\varepsilon$ gives $\lambda=0.523$ at $\varepsilon=2^{-52}$; their bound's exponent is four times weaker ($e^{-\pi^2/(4\lambda^2)}$) because it comes from an upper bound rather than the projection identity, so their $\lambda^\ast$ is half ours. The measured Gaussian wall in expC07 sat at our value. So the fiber identity sharpens their constant by a factor of two for the Gaussian; their framework supplies the theorem structure ours lacked.

What they do not cover: kernels that are integrals of a bump (tanh $r=1$; gelu, swish $r=2$), the $(\theta/2\pi)^r$ attenuation, the exact (rather than bounded) floor, and any activation-specific table.

## 2. The saturation (aliasing) error is classical

- V. Maz'ya, G. Schmidt, "On approximate approximations using Gaussian kernels", IMA J. Numer. Anal. 16, 13–29 (1996), and the book *Approximate Approximations* (AMS 2007): quasi-interpolation with Gaussians on a lattice converges to high order "up to a saturation error negligible in numerical applications", of the form $e^{-\pi^2\mathcal D}$ in their scaling. This is the interpolant-side floor (our Theorem 3 of the theory doc, $|1-M|\approx2A$).
- J. P. Boyd, "Error saturation in Gaussian radial basis functions on a finite interval", J. Comput. Appl. Math. 234, 1435–1441 (2010): for fixed $\alpha$ (shape parameter in grid units) the error does not go to zero as $h\to0$ but saturates at $\propto e^{-\pi^2/\alpha^2}$, and the saturation is much larger on a finite interval than on the infinite lattice because the interval "is equivalent to an infinite grid with a gap of many points". J. P. Boyd and L. Wang, "An analytic approximation to the cardinal functions of Gaussian radial basis functions on an infinite lattice", Appl. Math. Comput. 215, 2215–2223 (2009): cardinal-function error $4e^{-\pi^2/\alpha^2}$. Boyd's $e^{-\pi^2/\alpha^2}$ is our $A(\lambda)$ for the Gaussian, and his finite-interval degradation is the halo question.
- M. Unser, "Sampling — 50 years after Shannon", Proc. IEEE 88, 569–587 (2000); T. Blu, M. Unser, "Quantitative Fourier analysis of approximation techniques, Part I", IEEE Trans. Signal Process. 47, 2783–2795 (1999): the least-squares approximation error in a shift-invariant space is $\frac1{2\pi}\int|\hat f|^2E(\omega)d\omega$ with the error kernel $E=1-|\hat\phi|^2/\sum_k|\hat\phi(\omega+2\pi k)|^2$. That is our $\Lambda_0$; the theory doc already cites it. Our $\Lambda_r$ for $r\ge1$ is the extension to integrated generators.

## 3. The trade-off and its removal

- R. Schaback, "Error estimates and condition numbers for radial basis function interpolation", Adv. Comput. Math. 3, 251–264 (1995): the "uncertainty principle": attainable error and condition number cannot both be small. This is the two-wall picture for interpolation.
- The trade-off was later shown to be a property of the *basis*, not the problem: T. A. Driscoll, B. Fornberg, "Interpolation in the limit of increasingly flat radial basis functions", Comput. Math. Appl. 43, 413–422 (2002) (flat limit is polynomial interpolation); B. Fornberg, G. Wright, Contour-Padé (2004); B. Fornberg, C. Piret, RBF-QR on the sphere (SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 30, 2007/8); B. Fornberg, E. Larsson, N. Flyer, "Stable computations with Gaussian radial basis functions", SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 33, 869–892 (2011); G. Fasshauer, M. McCourt, "Stable evaluation of Gaussian radial basis function interpolants", SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 34, A737–A762 (2012) (Hilbert–Schmidt SVD); Fornberg–Lehto–Powell RBF-GA (2013); Wright–Fornberg RBF-RA (2017). These compute the small-shape-parameter limit stably in double precision by changing basis. For our problem this says the left wall of the Gaussian bump basis is removable by a change of basis; whether the same holds for the tanh basis (integrated $\mathrm{sech}^2$, positive-definite kernel) is not settled here but is the natural question.
- R. Platte, L. N. Trefethen, A. Kuijlaars, "Impossibility of fast stable approximation of analytic functions from equispaced samples", SIAM Review 53, 308–318 (2011): any method converging exponentially from equispaced samples is exponentially ill-conditioned; stable methods get at most root-exponential convergence. Our samples are equispaced but oversampled relative to the centers; the Adcock–Huybrechs frame theory is the positive counterpart (accuracy of order the threshold with bounded coefficients), and the extended impossibility theorem in Adcock–Shadrin (2021/2023) relates achievable accuracy to conditioning for finite-accuracy methods.

## 4. Solvers at other precisions

- Emulation: N. J. Higham, S. Pranesh, "Simulating low precision floating-point arithmetic", SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 41, C585–C602 (2019), the MATLAB `chop`; M. Fasi, M. Mikaitis, CPFloat (ACM TOMS 2023); `pychop` (Python, NumPy/PyTorch/JAX backends, arXiv:2504.07835). The standard is to round after **every** operation, including inside the factorization. The `precision_ladder.py` emulation in this folder rounded inputs, coefficients, the forward-pass partial sums and applied the cutoff, but not the solver's internal arithmetic, and it failed validation against native fp32 by a factor 5–1000 in the floor. That is consistent with these references: the solver's internal roundoff is the dominant fp32 mechanism here and must be emulated operation by operation.
- Mixed precision for least squares: E. Carson, N. J. Higham, S. Pranesh, "Three-precision GMRES-based iterative refinement for least squares problems", SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 42, A4063–A4083 (2020); N. J. Higham, T. Mary, "Mixed precision algorithms in numerical linear algebra", Acta Numerica 31, 347–414 (2022). These recover higher-precision least-squares accuracy from a low-precision factorization by iterative refinement; if fp32 solves are the practical target, this is the tool, not a $\lambda$ rule.
- Beyond fp64: no native format on this machine (`longdouble` is 64-bit on arm64). Arbitrary precision (mpmath, or the MPLAPACK / double-double libraries) is the only route; the small mpmath run in `mp_beyond_fp64.py` is the local attempt.

## 5. What this means for the hardened rule

- The scaling law ($\lambda$ constant, $\gamma\propto N$), the halo, the oversampled least squares and the truncated SVD are all in Adcock–Huybrechs–Piret with proofs for the Gaussian; the paper should cite it as the closest prior work and position the fiber floor as the exact, integrated-kernel refinement of their bound.
- The two-wall idea has a rigorous home: their eq. (7) (frame approximation error $=\inf_z\{\text{approximation}+\tau\|z\|\}$). A corrected two-wall note would restate the rule in that form rather than with a band-edge proxy and a declared $c$.
- The left wall is, for Gaussians, a basis artifact removable by RBF-QR-type algorithms; the fp32 story may therefore be a solver story, and mixed-precision refinement is the established remedy.
- Boyd's finite-interval saturation is the theoretical counterpart of the halo ladder.
