I prefer **Document B, “Why small tanh slopes make readout gradient descent slow,” as the explanation for an ML research audience**. It more directly answers why changing gamma changes convergence under the learning-rate normalization actually used. Document A has the stronger presentation of error-controlled trajectory prediction and validation against executed GD. Those are different strengths; neither theorem subsumes the other.

I read all six pages of A and all fourteen pages of B, including their appendices, and inspected the rendered figures and principal derivations. This assessment uses only those PDFs. I did not inspect source code, archives, other notes, or prior reviews, and I did not independently reproduce their numerical results.

My preference rests on five points:

1. **B identifies the relevant training quantity before explaining gamma.** Sections 1–2, pp. 1–2, derive the residual recurrence and show that, with the stated curvature-normalized step, each component decays according to its eigenvalue ratio, weighted by the target's projection. This immediately separates uniform kernel rescaling, changes in spectral shape, and changes in target alignment. A's periodic argument is clear, but its exponential optimization penalty is initially stated at a fixed step size (p. 1), whereas its experiments use a different curvature-normalized step at each gamma (Section 4, p. 3). A discloses this correctly; B makes the normalization part of the mechanism from the outset.

2. **B proves a more directly comparative statement about gamma in a finite network.** Equation (7), p. 4, expresses the change in the translation-invariant part as a nonnegative Fourier integral for every finite sample vector. Theorem 1 then retains the boundary and grid corrections and uses the old leading eigenspace to lower-bound a new normalized eigenvalue (p. 5; Appendix C, p. 10). The reported rank-32 comparison is especially useful: its lower bound exceeds the old ratio by about 690 times. That number remains a floating-point evaluation of the proved inequality. A's Theorem 1 instead constructs an approximation at each gamma and transfers spectral energy and residual estimates to the original matrix (pp. 2, 5–6). This is valuable, but it does not itself establish a change in normalized finite-network rates between two gammas. For explaining that change, B's theorem is the closer match.

3. **B makes the practical distinction between an available fit and slow optimization particularly concrete.** Section 4, pp. 6–7, combines a positive-rate band with substantial target energy, a necessary-step bound, and an explicitly evaluated readout whose error is below the desired tolerance. Its extension to every scalar step schedule within the specified stability range strengthens the interpretation: the example is not explained away by modestly increasing the chosen constant step (Appendix D, p. 11). A also carefully excludes nullspace energy and proves representability of the isolated slow component; its executed crossings establish practical relevance. I nevertheless find B's explicit attainable fit and schedule bound especially effective for an ML reader asking whether capacity or ordinary GD is the obstacle.

4. **B makes its explanatory limitations unusually inspectable.** The rank-paired target-weight control (Figure 1, p. 2) supports the importance of changing ratios in the selected experiment, while Appendix E.3, p. 13, explains why it is not a unique causal decomposition when eigenvectors rotate. Appendix D explicitly distinguishes a ratio lower bound from a necessary training-time bound; Figure 4's caption states that the full-target forecast comes from a separate integral-spectrum calculation. The exact-versus-measured table on p. 13 is useful. A also has careful caveats, but its short main theorem introduces the constructed kernel, error allowance, cumulative-energy bounds, buffers, and positive-band subtraction together. B's additional space generally explains necessary transitions rather than merely adding material. Figure 2's matrix heatmaps contribute less than its other figures, so this is not an endorsement of every page.

5. **A wins on the evidence for accurate training forecasts, but that does not reverse my explanatory preference.** Its three-panel Figure 1, p. 4, is a compact and effective progression from smoothing to positive slow energy to measured training. The saved executed GD checkpoints and first crossings provide a check beyond comparing two spectral calculations. A also reports containment of independently certified archived timing intervals, while distinguishing that validation from the uncertified new band-energy numbers. B's full-curve agreement (Figure 5, p. 12), SVD checks, and quadrature refinement are useful, but both sides of its forecast comparison are numerical spectral calculations; the trillion-step trajectory was not executed. I give A this evidential advantage without treating its reported archive checks as independently verified by me.

On the mathematics, I found no substantive defect in either central argument from reading the proofs. A's geometric feature remainder, kernel perturbation, buffered projector transfer, and telescoping residual bound form a coherent error-control argument. B's center-integral identity, Fourier transform of the decaying difference, and subspace min–max argument also fit together. In particular, B does not incorrectly infer an eigenvalue change from a single old eigenvector's Rayleigh quotient, and neither note assumes finite-kernel eigenvectors remain Fourier waves.

**A's main weakness** is that the finite-network mechanism is still substantially a reconstruction-and-spectral-calculation procedure. The periodic multiplier supplies the immediate intuition; the theorem guarantees that the corrected calculation can faithfully recover the actual finite problem. It does not analytically establish which normalized finite rates improve, or by how much, as gamma changes. The note says so on p. 3, but that leaves a gap between the motivating exponential attenuation and a comparative finite-network explanation. Its numerical band claims also depend on a heuristic floating-point allowance whose concrete settings and sensitivity results are referred to another note (p. 4), so those particular “at least” numbers cannot be audited from this PDF alone.

**B's main weakness** is that its comparative theorem remains conditional on substantial finite-matrix information. The correction is evaluated using the new kernel, and useful improvement requires the resulting bound to beat the old ratio (pp. 5–6, 10). It supplies no gamma-only law and no analytic guarantee that corrections stay sufficiently small across a chosen family of geometries. Furthermore, the selected-rank theorem is not what predicts the reported full-target hitting times. The separate center-integral approximation does that, with observed rather than certified accuracy. Thus B supports a finite spectral mechanism more directly than A, but it does not complete a theorem-level derivation of the entire training-time improvement from gamma alone.

I would choose A instead if the immediate purpose were a short collaborator note centered on **reliable target-specific forecasting and measured GD agreement**. For the stated explanatory purpose, my preference would change if A added a comparably clear finite-network comparison under the actual step normalization, or if B's crucial numerical correction and ratio comparisons failed independent reproduction. Both would become more persuasive with certified numerical bounds for their highlighted slow-band energies; B would particularly benefit from connecting its comparative ratio theorem to bounds on the target components that actually determine the hitting time.
