# Scale acquisition: readout size, coupled motion, and a conditional barrier

Why can noiseless joint training fail to acquire the slope scales needed for
the intended approximation regime? The evidence identifies more than one
failure regime. Degree 9 has an exceptionally weak target force and mostly
corrects errors generated by the network. Sine develops a much stronger force
but still fails to produce enough broadly distributed scale growth. A theory
for sine must explain the coupled evolution, rather than inherit degree 9's
weak-force premise.

The frozen-geometry check adds a useful fact: at large construction slopes,
both GD and Adam learn sine readouts consistent with $O(h)$, where $h$ is
center spacing. This supports asking whether readout adaptation obstructs the
path to that geometry. It does not prove such an obstruction. Our strongest
current theorem establishes finite-time persistence from specified degree-9
checkpoints; an equally predictive result for sine remains open.

**Notation. The model is a sum of tanh features, with no division by width.**

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $a_j,b_j,c_j,d$ | Slope, hidden bias, readout of neuron $j$, and output bias. |
| $\theta=(a,b,c,d)$, $W$ | All parameters and number of neurons. |
| $\gamma_j=\lvert a_j\rvert$ | Slope magnitude. In a frozen construction dictionary, all slopes equal a positive $\gamma$. |
| $h=2/N$, $\tau_j$ | Spacing and centers of a construction dictionary; $W$ also includes halo neurons. |
| $r=f_\theta-y$, $L=\Vert r\Vert_m^2/2$ | Residual and empirical half-MSE; the inner product uses a mean over training points. |
| $g_c=\nabla_cL$, $g_a=\nabla_aL$ | Readout and slope gradients. Lowercase $c$ means readout parameters. |
| $q_k,e_k$ | Degree-$k$ empirical orthonormal polynomial and residual coefficient $e_k=\langle q_k,r\rangle_m$. |
| $C,H$ in subscripts | Coarse modes 0–1 and higher-degree modes. Uppercase $C$ does not mean readout weights. |
| $F_a=T_ae_H$ | Effective fine slope gradient after accounting for instantaneous coarse balance. Its contribution to velocity is $-F_a$. |
| $\eta$ | GD step in the physical parameters; all blocks use the same rate in the main D34 comparison. |

Each numbered section follows **one example → the theory explaining it → a
prediction**. Section 1 identifies the general question. Sections 2–3 examine
the readout-scale hypothesis. Section 4 explains the theorem we can currently
prove. Section 5 states what a transport theory still needs to explain for
sine. Full proofs and the earlier evidence remain in the
[detailed companion](d34_coarse_balance_stagnation_details.md).

## 1. A stronger signal can coexist with failed acquisition

**Example: sine recovers force without reaching the intended regime.** In the
five width-177 sine trajectories, the effective slope-force norm grows by a
median factor of 12.8 between updates 400k and 600k. Yet at 600k the median
mean gamma is only 0.158 and relative evaluation MSE is about 0.496. A frozen
Jacobian forecast from 100k has 99.4% median force-vector error at 600k.
Thus a fixed weak sensitivity cannot explain this interval. These measurements
come from the [force audit](../results/checkpoint_D_optimizers/expD34_readout_race/force_plateaus/README.md)
and [target comparison](../results/checkpoint_D_optimizers/expD34_readout_race/adam_force_extension/README.md).

**Theory: GD follows the current residual through an evolving sensitivity.**
The model and its slope gradient are

$$
f_\theta(x)=d+\sum_jc_j\tanh(a_jx+b_j),\qquad
(g_a)_j=c_j\langle r,x\operatorname{sech}^2(a_jx+b_j)\rangle_m.
$$

A large readout multiplies a slope sensitivity, but the residual determines
the resulting gradient. To describe the residual's shape, orthogonalize
$1,x,x^2,\ldots$ on the training grid and project $r$ onto the resulting
polynomials. The coefficient $e_3$ measures a cubic-shaped error. It is not
an error cubed or a Taylor approximation to the network. All measurements
use exact tanh outputs.

Coarse balance accounts for the constant and linear errors adjusting to
ongoing fine fitting. After this adjustment, the slope force is

$$
F_a=T_ae_H=T_a(\varphi_H-Y_H),
$$

where $\varphi_H$ and $Y_H$ are network and target coefficients. The raw
gradient additionally includes coarse disequilibrium and any omitted-residual
force. Those corrections are small in the measured GD regime. They are
distinct from $g_c$, the full readout gradient. The
[balance derivation](d34_coarse_balance_stagnation_details.md#1-what-small-readout-disequilibrium-actually-controls)
defines the maps precisely.

Three quantities can therefore change: sensitivity to the target, the residual
left after the current fit, and the direction of the resulting slope force.
For positive $a_j$, a positive gradient shrinks the slope. Force magnitude
alone does not say which way a neuron moves.

There is also an important target distinction. A pure degree-9 fine target
has zero quadratic and cubic coefficients, so those errors are unwanted
network-generated output. Sine has a nonzero cubic target coefficient.
Correcting its cubic residual cannot automatically be described as removing
an unwanted cubic output.

**Prediction.** A useful sine surrogate must reproduce force recovery and
signed outward movement while evolving its own residual and sensitivities.
Matching only a force norm, or importing the degree-9 frozen map, is
insufficient. A model predicting persistent inward mean motion throughout
the sine recovery interval would contradict the observed trajectory.

## 2. Large construction slopes do lead to small learned readouts

**Example: freeze a sine dictionary at $N=128$, $\gamma=32$.** Its center
spacing is $h=1/64$. Reset the readouts and output bias to zero, then train
only those parameters for 600k updates. GD at rate 0.002, Adam at 0.002,
and Adam at 0.0002 all produce an interior readout RMS of approximately
$2.245h$. No norm constraint is imposed. The previous “Adam geometry” assay
used readout GD; the [new check](../results/checkpoint_D_optimizers/expD34_readout_race/readout_scale/README.md)
actually runs both readout optimizers.

**Theory: the coefficients discretize a density.** At large positive gamma,
$\tanh(\gamma(x-\tau))$ approaches a step whose jump is 2. A change of
approximately $h y'(\tau_j)$ between adjacent locations therefore calls
for a coefficient near

$$
c_j\approx\frac h2y'(\tau_j).
$$

For sine this is $c_j\approx\pi h\cos(2\pi\tau_j)$. The finite-gamma
interior construction includes the factor
$\sinh(\pi^2/\gamma)/(\pi^2/\gamma)$; the measured interior profiles
are close to that density. Boundary corrections and the output bias must
be considered separately.

The assertion $c_j=O(h)$ concerns individual physical weights. If a bounded
density is sampled at $W=O(1/h)$ centers, its hidden-readout norms instead
scale as $\|c\|_1=O(1)$ and $\|c\|_2=O(\sqrt h)$. A total norm including
the output bias would obscure this comparison. Also, the construction
provides an accurate representation with these weights; it does not prove
that every accurate representation must have the same weights.

**Prediction and check.** Refining $h$ at fixed $\gamma h$ should keep
the normalized weight profile and interior RMS/$h$ comparable. For
$\gamma h=0.5$, the measured values are:

**Interior readout RMS divided by $h$ at 600k updates, for the same sine target and zero initialization.**

| $N$ | $\gamma$ | GD, 0.002 | Adam, 0.002 | Adam, 0.0002 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 64 | 16 | 2.34097 | 2.34071 | 2.34077 |
| 128 | 32 | 2.24514 | 2.24515 | 2.24517 |
| 256 | 64 | 2.22447 | 2.22450 | 2.22447 |

Across the full three-width, three-gamma comparison, interior RMS/$h$ is
2.218–2.802 and the largest individual weight is below $4.56h$. This supports
the proposed scale in these frozen fits. Adam's instantaneous loss can
oscillate even while this statistic remains stable, so the result is about
weight scale rather than convergence to a precision solution. Three widths
and one target do not establish an asymptotic law for either optimizer.

## 3. Does the changing readout scale create a barrier?

**Example: fitting one coefficient can favor a decreasing readout.** Take the
scalar model $f=c\gamma$ with target 1. At $\gamma=1$, the fitted readout is
$c=1$; at $\gamma=2$, it is $c=1/2$. Larger $c$ increases the derivative
$\partial f/\partial\gamma=c$, yet larger gamma needs smaller $c$ for the
same output. This is the apparent conflict in its simplest form. The whole
curve $c=1/\gamma$ has zero loss, however, so the two endpoint scales alone
do not imply an energy barrier. On that curve GD has no force to move along
it. This example illustrates a distinction, not a model of sine's unresolved
error.

**Theory: separate the fitted readout from the readout reached by training.**
For a frozen common-gamma dictionary, collect the hidden readouts and output
bias in $w=(c,d)$ and write $f=A(\gamma)w$. Suppose on an interval there is
a differentiable fitted-readout branch $w_*(\gamma)$, with a nonsingular
readout Hessian on the subspace being considered. Define

$$
\overline L(\gamma)=L(\gamma,w_*(\gamma)),\qquad
\delta w=w-w_*(\gamma),\qquad H=A^TA/m.
$$

The function $\overline L$ is the loss achievable along that branch. The
vector $\delta w$ measures readout lag. These are diagnostic objects; ordinary
GD does not install $w_*$ during training. Since the readout loss is quadratic,
equal-rate gradient flow gives the exact lag equation

$$
\dot{\delta w}=-H\delta w-w_*'(\gamma)\dot\gamma.
$$

The first term removes lag. The second creates lag because the fitted readout
changes when gamma changes. Expanding the slope gradient near that branch gives

$$
\dot\gamma=-\overline L'(\gamma)
-\partial^2_{\gamma w}L(\gamma,w_*)\,\delta w
+O(\|\delta w\|^2).
$$

This identifies two different possible obstructions. Even with accurate
readout tracking, the profiled loss may offer little outward drive. Or readout
lag may oppose an outward profiled gradient. It can also reinforce that
gradient: the mixed derivative determines the sign. A decreasing preferred
readout is therefore a motivation for studying this coupling, not a proof
that the two parameter blocks are pursuing conflicting objectives.

There is a more specific slowdown mechanism when the lag adjusts quickly
enough to remain approximately steady. Motion then has to carry the changing
readout along with the geometry. Differentiating the fitted-readout condition
$\nabla_wL(\gamma,w_*)=0$ gives
$\partial^2_{\gamma w}L=-w_*'^TH$. Setting the lag equation approximately
to zero gives $H\delta w\approx-w_*'\dot\gamma$. Substituting into the
slope equation yields

$$
\bigl(1+\|w_*'(\gamma)\|^2\bigr)\dot\gamma
\approx-\overline L'(\gamma).
$$

Thus a rapidly changing fitted readout can slow gamma motion even when the
profiled loss decreases outward. This is a conditional local approximation:
it needs small lag and fast enough lag relaxation, neither of which the
frozen endpoint experiment checks. A readout learning rate $\kappa$ times
the geometry rate replaces the added factor by $\|w_*'\|^2/\kappa$.
The relevant quantity is how steeply the fitted readout changes along the
path, not just its final $O(h)$ size. Small eigenvalues of $H$ can instead
invalidate steady tracking and require keeping the full lag dynamics.

The actual D34 geometry includes independent slopes and biases, rather than
one gamma. The same calculation has a geometry vector and mixed Hessian
blocks. Its use requires checking the readout branch and its conditioning;
near-null readout directions make an unqualified inverse unreliable. Small
coarse disequilibrium does not imply small full-readout lag.

Finally, the coordinate choice matters. D34 differentiates $a_j$ with $b_j$
independent. If instead a center $\tau_j$ is fixed and $b_j=-\gamma_j\tau_j$,
then

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\gamma_j}\Big|_{\tau_j}
=c_j(x-\tau_j)\operatorname{sech}^2(\gamma_j(x-\tau_j)).
$$

This differs from the raw slope derivative in Section 1. Localized features,
small readouts, and the parameter metric all affect the speed of scale
changes. The frozen-readout check does not determine that speed.

**Prediction.** If harmful readout lag blocks acquisition, improving tracking
of the fitted branch should restore an outward force that was previously
canceled. If even the fitted branch has a weak outward gradient, better
tracking will not restore it. A third possibility is that faster fitting
removes an accessible residual that was driving geometry, shortening the
outward episode. These alternatives can be separated by measuring the
profiled force, actual force, and their signed difference at the same geometry,
then testing an autonomous change in readout rate. Detached readout solves
are diagnostics; using them as training updates would be a separate intervention.
Where steady tracking is valid, the sharper prediction is a speed reduction
by the factor $1+\|w_*'\|^2/\kappa$. Measuring a large endpoint change
without a large local derivative would not support this particular mechanism.

## 4. What the degree-9 theorem actually proves

**Example: most remaining loss is locally unavailable.** At the ten degree-9
checkpoints used for persistence forecasts, the remaining half-MSE is about
0.375. Nevertheless, in the selected parameter neighborhoods only
$7.74\times10^{-7}$ to $1.52\times10^{-6}$ of loss can be removed before
leaving the neighborhood. This gap, rather than the full remaining loss,
is what produces a long movement bound. It is a result about these stalled
states, not an assumption inherited from the sine experiment.

**Theory: a local loss floor bounds the movement that GD can sustain.** Choose
a ball of radius $R$ about the starting state $\theta_s$. Small-slope tanh
features have small ninth-degree coefficients throughout a sufficiently small
ball, including the allowed readout changes. If the network's hard-output
coefficient is bounded by $H_R$, then

$$
L(\theta)\ge\ell_R:=\tfrac12[|Y_9|-H_R]_+^2,\qquad
\Delta_R=L(\theta_s)-\ell_R.
$$

The first quantity is the local loss floor; the second is the loss available
to spend on movement. Let $U_R$ bound the loss Hessian norm throughout the
ball and let $q_R=1-\eta U_R/2>0$. GD descent and Cauchy–Schwarz give

$$
\eta q_R\sum_{k<n}\|g_k\|^2\le\Delta_R,\qquad
\sum_{k<n}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k\|
\le Q_n:=\sqrt{\frac{\eta n\Delta_R}{q_R}}.
$$

Here $g_k$ is the full parameter gradient and $n$ counts additional updates.
These estimates first hold for steps inside the ball. To justify that
condition, reserve an inner radius

$$
r_R=\frac{R-\eta\|g_s\|}{1+\eta U_R}>0.
$$

If previous travel is below $r_R$, curvature bounds put the next entire step
inside $R$. The loss estimate then bounds the new travel by $Q_n$. Whenever
$Q_n<r_R$, induction keeps all steps inside. If $Q_n$ is also smaller than
the slope displacement required for an acquisition event, the event cannot
occur during those $n$ updates. The
[full derivation](d34_coarse_balance_stagnation_details.md#9-from-a-force-decomposition-to-a-persistence-prediction)
gives the hard-output estimate, curvature constants, and population distance.

This theorem does not require a frozen actual Jacobian, mean contraction, or
noise. It does require the local floor and curvature bounds. Neither follows
from small coarse disequilibrium alone. The two-error surrogate explains the
observed motion; the loss-floor theorem separately bounds its possible travel.

**Prediction and check.** Evaluating the conditions in FP64 gives horizons
of 10.2–17.1 million additional updates across the ten checkpoints, with every
gamma below 0.388. Full GD has been simulated through six million updates and
remains far from the intended geometry and precision. The longer horizons
are predictions of the evaluated theorem, not trajectories that were run.
These are ordinary floating-point evaluations, not directed-rounding numerical
certificates. The [evidence](d34_coarse_balance_stagnation_details.md#10-testing-what-keeps-the-force-small)
does not establish entry from initialization or permanent trapping, and does
not transfer these numerical horizons to sine.

## 5. What the transport theory still needs to explain for sine

**Example: outward motion is unevenly distributed.** In the original sine
runs, over updates 20k–600k the largest 10% of neurons carry a median 48.6%
of all outward gamma travel. At 600k, no seed has more than one neuron above
gamma 3.2. This is appreciable but insufficient scale movement, accompanied
by large remaining approximation error. The
[movement report](../results/checkpoint_D_optimizers/expD34_readout_race/signal_recovery/README.md)
records both inward and outward travel at every update.

**Theory: the shared residual drives a distribution of unequal responses.**
Let $\rho=W^{-1}\sum_j\delta_{(a_j,b_j,c_j)}$ be the neuron population.
Under gradient flow it satisfies the continuity equation

$$
\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho v)=0,
\qquad v=-(g_a,g_b,g_c).
$$

Every neuron sees the same residual, but its sensitivity depends on its
slope, bias, and readout. Fitting by one part of the population changes the
force seen by the rest. The fitted-readout branch from Section 3 is one
way to organize that feedback; the distribution must still determine the
changing residual and sensitivities. There is no stochastic escape term.
Simultaneous GD moves the atoms by the old-state velocities times $\eta$.

For any neuron beginning below a threshold $\Gamma$, reaching it requires
enough cumulative outward travel. Define that quantity for GD by

$$
P_{j,+}=\sum_n[|a_{j,n+1}|-|a_{j,n}|]_+.
$$

It counts outward motion even when later inward motion cancels it. A
population-acquisition theorem must predict the distribution of these budgets,
or a bound on their total. The measured budgets already exclude specified
population events on the original interval; explaining why they remain small
before seeing the trajectory is the unresolved step for sine.

**Prediction.** If a small growing subset removes the residual needed by
the rest, suppressing that subset's fitting contribution should prolong or
increase outward forcing on the other neurons after coarse transients settle.
If concentration is instead primarily due to unequal sensitivities, such
an intervention need not redistribute the motion. The readout-lag test in
Section 3 distinguishes another possibility. These are competing causal
predictions, not conclusions of the current frozen fits. A successful theory
must reproduce sine's observed force recovery while explaining its limited
population travel and poor approximation accuracy.
