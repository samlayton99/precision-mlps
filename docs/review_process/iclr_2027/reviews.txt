Paper Summary
-------------

The paper investigates whether exact numerical computation can be built directly into the
weights of ordinary neural networks, instead of relying on chain-of-thought or external tools.
It proposes QUILL, a recipe for setting the weights of a one-hidden-layer  network so that it
computes a chosen smooth one-variable function (for example , , exp) to near the limit of 64-bit
floating point.

Presentation: 2: Generally understandable, but requires substantial effort in places.

Informativeness: 3: I gained a substantially deeper understanding of the problem or question.

Soundness: 2: I have minor or fixable concerns about the paper’s soundness.

Critical Strengths
------------------

- The paper provides a clearer understanding of why high-precision computation is difficult for
  neural networks. In particular, it shows a tradeoff between network width, weight magnitude,
  and numerical precision, and how a one-hidden-layer construction can balance all three.

- The paper proposes a single-layer construction that is very accurate, uses small weights, and
  works with standard computer precision.

- The handling of rounding errors is thorough and well validated.

- The experiments convincingly show errors around  across many functions.

- The observation that accurate small networks exist but normal training does not find them is
  interesting.

Critical Weaknesses
-------------------

- The following claims in the paper are stronger than what the experimental results demonstrate.

  -  The paper says it proves that gradient descent cannot learn the right network fast enough.
    The proof only holds under conditions checked experimentally for limited training periods,
    and stronger training methods are not tested.

  -  The experiments use a different fitting method from the one analyzed in the theory.

- The LLM demo doesn’t show what the paper suggests. The input number is read by hand-written
  code, and the answer is written back by hand-written code. The module also runs in FP64 while
  the model runs in BF16. So the language model is not doing any of the computation. Rather, it
  works more like a calculator plugged into the forward pass. Because of this, the comparison
  with CoT and calculator use in Table 3 is not fair.

- Precision is a problem for LLMs. The paper’s own Figure 2c shows that accuracy drops a lot at
  lower precision. FP64 precision itself only holds about 15–16 digits, so it is not clear if
  the proposed method would work beyond 7-digit numbers.

- It is not clear if the neural network form is needed for one-variable functions. Standard
  methods like Chebyshev approximation already reach this accuracy easily. The main reason given
  for wanting it as a network is the LLM use case, which isn’t really shown.

- The paper states it proves gradient descent can’t learn these networks, but the result depends
  on conditions that are only checked experimentally for limited training runs. Some of the
  difficulty may simply be that the fitting problem is numerically hard. Michaud et al. (2023)
  trained networks close to machine precision with better optimizers, and that paper isn’t
  cited.

- The paper argues construction is necessary because training fails, but only tests GD and Adam.
  Stronger optimizers such as L-BFGS, used by Michaud et al. (2023) to reach near machine
  precision, should be compared.

Questions For Authors
---------------------

- What real benefit does the neural-network form give compared with standard high-precision
  methods run on a computer?

- In the language-model demo, what is the worst-case error over all 3-, 5- and 7-digit inputs?
  What exactly does the hand-written input reader do, and does it differ between the three
  models?

- How well does the module work at the language model’s own precision (16-bits)?

- Can the method handle numbers longer than 7 digits?

- How do stronger training methods, such as L-BFGS and the methods of Michaud et al., compare
  with the proposed construction?

Paper Summary
-------------

The paper proposes to extend MLPs with function blocks computing exact functions like x+y, x^2,
c*x, which in principle can improve accuracy of the neural networks (especially LLMs) on these
tasks.

Presentation: 2: Generally understandable, but requires substantial effort in places.

Informativeness: 1: I learned little or nothing new from this paper.

Soundness: 1: I have significant concerns about the paper’s soundness that do not appear easy to
address.

Critical Strengths
------------------

- Extending neural networks with exact mathematical functions in interesting, because it can not
  only improve the accuracy, which was primary motivation of authors, but also interpretability.

- The integration of the proposed module inside the LLM is indeed interesting.

Critical Weaknesses
-------------------

- I do not understand, what is the motivation of approximating known algebraic functions by MLPs
  in Equation (3).

- The demonstrated method works because authors have selected easy functions, like addition,
  multiplication, and square on a bounded interval. For this choice, the gradient is finite and
  everything converges. For more important functions, like division, this does not hold and
  everything becomes way more complicated. For example authors in [1] aims to extend neural
  networks with exact multiplication and division and the resulting unit is very unstable to
  train. Critically, this instability is not caused by chosen representation, but by the nature
  of function to be approximated.

- Line 393 states: "QUILL can approximate bounded primitives such as 1/x, sin(x), and 1/√x". To
  my knowledge, 1/x is unbounded around zero, which causes enormous issues when QUILL would be
  trained as a part of NN. Similarly 1/√x has unbounded derivative.

- Statement "QUILL can approximate bounded primitives" is obvious from the universal
  approximation theorem due to its form in Equation (3).

- The selected experimental case, x^2 is very simple and naive. The method in [1] can solve this
  with ease. It is trivial.

Reference
---------

[1] Heim, Niklas, Tomas Pevny, and Vasek Smidl. "Neural power units." Advances in Neural
Information Processing Systems 33 (2020): 6573-6583.

Questions For Authors
---------------------

Generally all my questions are in critical weakness.

Paper Summary
-------------

The paper propose a method for encoding bounded numerical functions in ordinary MLP weights. The
proposed method can assist LM to achieve better accuracy with lower token cost in numerical
functions computation compared to traditional solutions.

Presentation: 3: Clear and easy to follow.

Informativeness: 2: I learned something interesting or useful.

Soundness: 2: I have minor or fixable concerns about the paper’s soundness.

Critical Strengths
------------------

1. The proposed method can assist LM to achieve better accuracy with lower token cost in
   numerical function computations compared to using caculate, direct prompt and Chain of
   Thought.

2. This method enables the MLP component to achieve a lower  error whilst reducing the scale of
   hidden neurons.

Critical Weaknesses
-------------------

The proposed method encodes the target bounded numerical function into the weights of a standard
MLP, which appears to imply that QUILL must prepare specific MLP weights for different target
numerical functions. Although table 3 has demonstrated that the proposed approach can improve
accuracy and reduce token cost in  computation, the sacrifice of flexibility in general use
cases does not convince me to use QUILL to replace a calculator.

Questions For Authors
---------------------

I appreciate the author's efforts in this work and would kindly ask authors to clarify the
following questions:

1. Does QUILL require to prepare different MLP weights for different numerical functions?

2. If so, please clarify in which use cases it is worthwhile to sacrifice flexibility in
   exchange for improved accuracy and reduced token cost.
