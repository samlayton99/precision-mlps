\subsection{Bandwidth controls optimization access to high precision}
\label{sec:bandwidth-access}

The useful relative bandwidth $\lambda=\gamma h$ varies slowly with width
once the target is resolved (Figure~2b). Maintaining this bandwidth as
$h=\Theta(W^{-1})$ therefore requires $\gamma=\Theta(W)$.
Even though larger widths admit smaller $\lambda$ at comparable construction
error, small slopes can still delay readout learning by weakening
target-relevant kernel directions. We quantify this effect first with fixed
features, then examine whether joint training acquires useful feature geometry.

Consider the construction of Section~3.1, but solve its normalized readout least-squares
problem by gradient descent. The sampled feature matrix $\Phi_\gamma$ includes
the output bias; its kernel is $K_\gamma=\Phi_\gamma\Phi_\gamma^\top/M$,
where $M$ is the number of training points. If its eigenvalues are
$\mu_1\ge\cdots\ge\mu_M\ge0$, define
\begin{equation}
 \rho_i(\gamma)=\frac{\mu_i(K_\gamma)}{\mu_1(K_\gamma)}.
 \label{eq:note-relative-rates}
\end{equation}
The largest eigenvalue limits a stable shared step size; a small $\rho_i$
then makes the corresponding output component slow to learn.

\paragraph{How slope reaches these rates.}
A tanh feature changes over distance $1/\gamma$. At small slope, broad,
overlapping transitions respond weakly to rapidly alternating errors.
After removing the common constant component, the center-integrated kernel
is determined by the explicit pairwise profile
\begin{equation}
 -d\coth(\gamma d)
 =-|d|-\frac{2|d|}{e^{2\gamma|d|}-1},
 \qquad d=z_a-z_b,
 \label{eq:note-correlation-profile}
\end{equation}
with value $-1/\gamma$ at $d=0$. Here $z_a,z_b$ are any two training
points. The second term is the slope-dependent smoothing correction to the
step-feature profile $-|d|$. Bounding the discrepancy from the actual finite
center lattice transfers this explicit gamma dependence to the kernel spectrum.

\begin{theorem}[Slope-dependent relative kernel spectrum]
\label{thm:note-spectrum}
For the uniformly spaced centers and training points of Section~3.1 and a
common slope $\gamma>0$, the correlation profile
\eqref{eq:note-correlation-profile} yields computable bounds
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\underline\rho_i(\gamma)\le
 \frac{\mu_i(K_\gamma)}{\mu_1(K_\gamma)}
 \le\overline\rho_i(\gamma),\qquad i=1,\ldots,M.}
 \label{eq:note-spectrum-bounds}
\end{equation}
The endpoints depend only on the slope and fixed feature geometry, and lie
in $[0,1]$. Their explicit construction, finite-lattice and halo allowances,
and proof are given in Appendix~\ref{app:note-spectrum}.
\end{theorem}

Figure~\ref{fig:note-spectrum} makes this dependence quantitative: raising
$\lambda$ from $1/32$ to $1/4$ changes the fourth relative eigenvalue by
only a factor of $1.30$, but the thirtieth by $1.4\times10^5$.
The bounds follow this change by computing the spectrum of the explicit,
corrected correlation model before optimization. Whether these slow
directions matter for training depends on the target's projections.

\begin{corollary}[Output error from slow target components]
\label{cor:note-output}
For sampled target $y\ne0$, let $p_i(\gamma)=|u_i^\top y|^2/\|y\|^2$
be its energy fraction in kernel eigenvector $u_i$. For zero-initialized readout GD
with $\eta=1/(2\mu_1)$, the relative output error satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{e_n^2:=\frac{\|\Phi_\gamma w_n-y\|^2}{\|y\|^2}
 \ge\sum_i p_i(\gamma)
 \left(1-\frac{\overline\rho_i(\gamma)}2\right)^{2n}.}
 \label{eq:note-output-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

Indeed, the exact GD error is the same sum with $\rho_i$ in place of
$\overline\rho_i$. If directions carrying target energy $P$ have
$\overline\rho_i\le r$, they force $e_n\ge\sqrt P(1-r/2)^n$.
Small positive eigenvalues therefore delay components already representable
by the dictionary; they need not create a nonzero approximation floor.

\paragraph{Accurate readouts can remain slow to acquire.}
At $\lambda=1/32$, a direct readout fit achieves $6.2\times10^{-10}$
relative error on a dense evaluation grid. Yet GD retains training error
$0.210$ after five million updates; the corollary predicts at least
$0.2098$ (Figure~\ref{fig:note-training}A). At $\lambda=1/4$, GD reaches
$3.88\times10^{-4}$ over the same budget. Across the four bandwidths,
the lower bound is at least 75\% of executed error throughout training
and 91--100\% at the endpoint. Thus the gamma-dependent spectral bounds
quantify an optimization obstruction beyond attainable approximation error.

\paragraph{Joint training must acquire useful slopes and centers together.}
Joint Adam reaches median relative error $1.81\times10^{-3}$, whereas Adam
on the supplied $\lambda=1/4$ features reaches $6.62\times10^{-7}$ over
the same budget; joint GD remains at $0.244$
(Figure~\ref{fig:note-training}B). Adam's upper slope percentile approaches
the reference scale, but only 4--7 of 512 features reach it
(Figure~\ref{fig:note-training}C). A few steep features do not establish
that the useful geometry has been acquired. Moreover, refitting the learned
readout barely improves its error, while steepening the learned features
alone can worsen it. These observations motivate the next question: what
training signal moves slopes and centers into a jointly useful configuration?
The next analysis addresses this feature-acquisition step; the frozen theorem
isolates the readout obstruction once geometry is fixed.

The construction supplies both feature geometry and accurate readout weights,
avoiding these two optimization tasks. This motivates embedding the resulting
arithmetic primitives in larger networks. The underlying scale-dependent
spectral bias is consistent with prior
analysis~\citep{xu2020frequency,tancik2020fourier}; here its consequences are
quantified for the finite quasi-interpolant dictionary.
